# Composition and Layout

Use this reference when choosing, comparing, or repairing a spatial structure.

## Start with masses

Before styling, reduce the composition to grayscale masses:

- subject or image;
- headline;
- supporting copy;
- action or evidence;
- attribution;
- negative space.

Judge their relationship at reduced size. Fine typography cannot repair a composition whose dominant masses are unclear.

## Define one focal point

Create the focal point through a controlled difference in scale, luminance, isolation, saliency, position, direction, or distinctive shape. Secondary emphasis should support or lead toward it.

Two elements may form one unified focal relationship, such as a face plus headline. Avoid unrelated elements with equal weight.

## Use a small spacing system

When no Brand spacing system is pinned, a useful starting unit is:

```text
u = shorter canvas edge / 100
```

Start with a small subset such as `1u`, `2u`, `3u`, `5u`, and `8u`. Use smaller gaps within groups and larger gaps between groups. Apply optical adjustments only after geometric relationships are clear.

Canonical Brand spacing bindings and explicit Plan safe areas override this heuristic.

## Choose a grid that matches complexity

- Single field: one statement, image, or ceremonial centered composition.
- Four columns: compact social or simple poster structure.
- Six columns: editorial asymmetry and image-copy splits.
- Twelve columns: complex multi-module or presentation systems.
- Modular grid: repeated categories, data, schedules, or service menus.
- Baseline rhythm: sustained editorial typography.

A grid coordinates relationships. It does not require every element to occupy a visible cell. Record intentional exceptions instead of allowing near-alignments to drift.

## Declare alignment axes

Choose a primary axis such as a left edge, right edge, center, baseline, image edge, column boundary, or radial center. Add a secondary axis only for a distinct group.

First establish geometric alignment. Then make small optical corrections for curved letters, pointed shapes, logos, or irregular crops.

## Use negative space as structure

Negative space can separate groups, isolate the focal point, create a reading path, or establish tone. Do not fill it simply because it exists.

Too much unstructured space can disconnect related items. Judge the relationship created by the space, not the percentage of empty canvas.

## Balance visual weight

Weight increases with size, darkness, saturation, texture, complexity, isolation, semantic saliency, and edge tension.

At reduced size, ask:

- Is one quadrant unintentionally overloaded?
- Is the eye trapped in a corner?
- Does a secondary accent compete with the focal point?
- Is the center of gravity appropriate for the intended tension?

Balance may be asymmetric. A small high-contrast object can counter a large quiet field.

## Create a reading path

Use position, scale, contrast, alignment, repetition, gaze, and image motion to answer:

```text
What is noticed first?
What explains it?
What should happen next?
Where does the eye exit or return?
```

Select the path for the actual content and language direction.

## Control layering

Every overlap should communicate containment, foreground/background, readable underlay, depth, frame-breaking, masking, or annotation.

Ask which element is in front, why they intersect, and whether recognition or legibility is harmed. Keep canonical layer order intentional and front-to-back in Studio without changing semantic meaning.

## Avoid tangencies

When two edges nearly touch, choose one deliberate state:

- separate clearly;
- align exactly; or
- overlap decisively.

Pay special attention to text near frames, faces near text blocks, circular shapes near canvas edges, and nearly coincident rules.

## Construction sequence

1. Confirm canvas, safe area, and any full-bleed intent.
2. Choose a grid and alignment axes.
3. Block the primary mass.
4. Place comprehension support in a clear relationship.
5. Place action, evidence, and attribution.
6. Establish group spacing and negative space.
7. Check reading path and visual balance at reduced size.
8. Resolve overlaps, protected areas, and tangencies.
9. Apply optical corrections.
10. Add detailed typography, color, image treatment, and effects.

## Structural candidate rule

For consequential greenfield work, valid candidates should differ in at least two of:

- focal-point location;
- grid or column logic;
- image-to-copy proportion;
- alignment axis;
- reading path;
- grouping model;
- text-zone placement;
- layout archetype.

Color-only, font-only, and ornamental variations are not structural candidates.

## Structural review

A sound structure should have a nameable focal point, evident reading order, meaningful groups, repeated alignments, purposeful space, no accidental tangencies, and a plausible adaptation path. Reject the structure before styling when those relationships are unsound.
